Gratitude

The thinkers this work stands on.

The Field Trilogy and Fragile Light would not exist without the people on this page. Some of them know it. Most of them never will. What follows is an attempt at honest acknowledgment — who contributed what, where to read them, and why their work mattered to the books.

This is not a complete list. The full bibliography lives on the Reading page. This is the shorter, more personal list — the people without whom the trilogy's central architecture would have been impossible to assemble.

Every novel that takes itself seriously inherits more than it invents. The trilogy's claim — that consciousness is a field property, that brains are receivers, that the world we see is rendered at finite resolution from a deeper substrate — is not original. What is original, at most, is the way the claim has been narrated, the cases stitched into it, the chord José keeps coming back to. The argument itself was built, in pieces, by other people, over a century and more. The honest thing to do is name them.

The list below is organized by where each thinker's work enters the books. A few people appear in multiple sections because their work reaches into multiple domains. The order within each section is roughly chronological — older first, with the immediate predecessors of the trilogy's current shape near the end.

Physics & quantum foundations

Albert Einstein (1879–1955)

Asked the question Bell eventually answered. The 1935 EPR paper — co-written with Podolsky and Rosen — insisted that quantum mechanics could not be complete because it implied "spooky action at a distance." That objection forced the physics community to take entanglement seriously as a foundational issue, and set up the entire experimental programme that ran from Clauser through Aspect to Zeilinger. Einstein was wrong about what the experiments would show. He was right that the question deserved an answer.

John Stewart Bell (1928–1990)

The six-page paper that turned Einstein's philosophical objection into something a laboratory could test. Bell's theorem is the empirical floor under everything else on this site. Without Bell there would be no experimental quantum foundations, no 2022 Nobel Prize, no rigorous case that locality and realism cannot both be true. The trilogy's field cosmology rests entirely on the experimental closure of Bell's inequalities. Bell is the keystone.

Alain Aspect · John Clauser · Anton Zeilinger (2022 Nobel laureates)

The three experimentalists who closed Bell's question, decade by decade and loophole by loophole. Aspect's 1982 papers were the first decisive closure; Clauser had begun the programme a decade earlier; Zeilinger and others completed the loophole-closing work into the 2010s. The trilogy's confidence that nonlocality is empirical fact comes from their patient experimental work over forty years. Every entanglement scene in the books is downstream of their results.

John Archibald Wheeler (1911–2008)

The teacher of teachers. The delayed-choice experiment, the "participatory universe," the slogan it from bit. Wheeler argued more clearly than anyone else that observation is constitutive of physical reality at the quantum level — that the universe is not a stage on which observers happen to act, but a process partly made of observation. The trilogy's receiver model is a direct descendant of Wheeler's intuition that information and the observer are not optional features of physics.

David Bohm (1917–1992)

The pilot-wave reformulation, the implicate order, and the willingness to take metaphysics seriously while remaining a working physicist. Bohm's Wholeness and the Implicate Order is one of two or three books that the trilogy's substrate-and-rendering picture is most directly traceable to. The notion that the visible world is the "explicate" surface of a deeper "implicate" order, and that consciousness participates in the implicate level, is Bohm's. The books inherit it almost intact.

Yakir Aharonov (b. 1932)

The two-state vector formalism, weak values, the three-box paradox, and the case for time-symmetric quantum mechanics. Aharonov is the patient physicist who has been showing since the 1960s that quantum mechanics works better when read in both temporal directions. The trilogy's symmetric-300-ms architecture — readiness potential on one side, Lucía Reyes's cymatic pre-event window on the other — is built on his framework. The TSVF page walks through it.

Roger Penrose (b. 1931)

The number 1 in 1010123 — the precision the universe's initial conditions required — comes from Penrose. So does the Orch-OR proposal for quantum collapse in microtubules, the conformal cyclic cosmology, and the steady half-century insistence that consciousness is not algorithmic. Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind (1989) and The Road to Reality (2004) are foundational reading for any serious engagement with the questions this site is built around.

Jacob Bekenstein · Leonard Susskind · Gerard 't Hooft · Juan Maldacena

The holographic principle and ER = EPR. Bekenstein's 1981 entropy bound seeded what 't Hooft and Susskind generalized into the holographic principle in the 1990s. Maldacena's AdS/CFT correspondence (1997) is the mathematically rigorous realization of holography that has reshaped contemporary fundamental physics. The 2013 ER = EPR conjecture, with Susskind, made the radical claim explicit: spacetime itself may be woven by entanglement. The trilogy's claim that locality is rendered, not fundamental, sits inside this work directly.

James Gates Jr. (b. 1950)

Discovered, while doing pure-mathematical supersymmetry research, that the equations describing certain supersymmetric quantum field theories contain structures formally identical to error-correcting codes. The Adinkra diagrams encode actual computer-science error-correction. Gates did not set out to argue the universe is computed; the codes emerged unbidden from the equations. The discovery sits inside the simulation-hypothesis conversation as one of its most precise pointers.

Carlo Rovelli · Lee Smolin · Sabine Hossenfelder

The loop-quantum-gravity tradition and its descendants. Rovelli and Smolin's 1995 paper proved that area and volume operators in loop quantum gravity have discrete spectra — geometry itself comes in quanta. Hossenfelder's 2013 review walks through every independent argument for the Planck scale as a fundamental floor on physical resolution. The trilogy's universe-as-rendered-at-finite-resolution architecture is built on this work.

Federico Faggin · Giacomo Mauro D'Ariano · Gunnar Strømme

Contemporary physicists working at the edge of the consciousness-fundamental program. Faggin (who designed the world's first microprocessor) has spent the second half of his career arguing for consciousness as fundamental. D'Ariano's 2017 paper with Faggin is one of the cleanest formal statements of the receiver-model intuition in the physics literature. Strømme's phi-field cosmology is the most direct precursor of the trilogy's specific architecture. The books would have a very different shape without these three.

Consciousness, the hard problem, and the receiver-model tradition

William James (1842–1910)

The first modern philosopher of mind to take the "filter" or "receiver" idea seriously in print. James's late lectures argued explicitly that the brain may be more transmitter than producer — that consciousness comes through it rather than from it. Human Immortality (1898) is the founding text of the receiver-model tradition, predating the physics by a century.

Henri Bergson (1859–1941)

The filter theory of perception. Bergson argued in Matter and Memory (1896) that the brain selects and filters from a much wider field of awareness — that perception is reduction, not construction. Aldous Huxley picked the argument up half a century later in The Doors of Perception. The trilogy's receiver model inherits the filtering insight from this lineage.

Aldous Huxley (1894–1963)

The Doors of Perception (1954) reframed Bergson's filter theory for a twentieth-century audience and made it part of the cultural conversation. Huxley's insistence that the brain narrows rather than generates awareness is the literary ancestor of the trilogy's receiver model. Limen in particular owes Huxley a direct debt.

Karl Pribram (1919–2015)

The holonomic brain theory. Pribram, working alongside Bohm, proposed that the brain operates on holographic principles — distributed encoding, interference patterns, the whole present in each part. The neuroscience version of Bohm's implicate order. Pribram is the bridge between the physics and the biology of the receiver model.

Thomas Nagel (b. 1937)

What Is It Like to Be a Bat? (1974). The single most influential paper on consciousness in the second half of the twentieth century. Nagel made it impossible to confuse third-person descriptions of cognitive function with first-person experience. The hard problem, as Chalmers later named it, was already implicit in Nagel's 1974 framing.

David Chalmers (b. 1966)

The 1995 paper that named and clarified the hard problem of consciousness. Chalmers separated the easy problems (cognitive function) from the hard problem (felt experience) with such precision that the philosophy of mind has had to take the distinction seriously ever since. The trilogy's hard-problem essay is essentially Chalmers re-read in light of the receiver model. We could not have asked the question without him.

Stuart Hameroff (b. 1947)

The biological half of Orch-OR. Hameroff's 1990s collaboration with Penrose proposed quantum computation in neuronal microtubules as the substrate of consciousness. Controversial then, less controversial now — see the Bandyopadhyay companion for the experimental status. The trilogy treats microtubule coherence as a serious candidate mechanism, not as the only one.

Henry Stapp (b. 1928)

Mind, Matter, and Quantum Mechanics. Stapp argued for half a century that the conscious observer's role in quantum measurement is real, not metaphorical, and that consciousness is woven into the structure of physical law. His later work on quantum interactionism is one of the most direct precursors of the trilogy's coupling between the field and the brain.

Donald Hoffman (b. 1955)

The interface theory of perception. Hoffman's argument from evolutionary game theory — that perception is shaped to maximize fitness, not to track truth — converges on the receiver model from a completely independent direction. His mathematical work on "conscious agents" as the fundamental ontology of the universe is structurally close to the trilogy's field hypothesis.

Bernardo Kastrup

The most rigorous contemporary defender of idealism. Kastrup's The Idea of the World (2019) and subsequent work make the case that consciousness is the only thing we can be certain exists, and that physical reality is best understood as appearance within consciousness rather than as a producer of it. The trilogy's receiver model differs from Kastrup's strong idealism in keeping the physical substrate as a coupler — but the philosophical groundwork is largely his.

Galen Strawson · Philip Goff

Contemporary defenders of panpsychism. Their work has made it respectable, after a century of taboo, for philosophers to argue that consciousness is fundamental and ubiquitous. The trilogy is not panpsychist in the strict sense — but the panpsychist tradition is the closest cousin and has done much of the philosophical groundwork the receiver model rests on.

Daniel Dennett (1942–2024)

The most rigorous defender of the eliminativist position, and the position the trilogy most directly contests. Dennett's Consciousness Explained (1991) and decades of follow-up work made the production-and-functionalist case better than anyone else. We owe Dennett the obligation to take that case seriously and to answer it on its own terms. The trilogy attempts that. Whether successfully is for the reader to judge.

Anomalous evidence and clinical research

Ian Stevenson (1918–2007)

Founded the University of Virginia Division of Perceptual Studies. Spent fifty years documenting cases of young children who appeared to remember previous lives with verifiable specific details, including birthmarks corresponding to fatal wounds in the previous personality. Two thousand cases, peer-reviewed, gathered with clinical care. Stevenson is, on the empirical evidence alone, one of the most underappreciated scientists of the twentieth century. The Lucía Reyes character in Anima is built on his methodology.

Jim Tucker

Stevenson's successor at UVA, continuing the past-life-cases program with the same standards. Tucker's Life Before Life (2005) and Return to Life (2013) are the contemporary updates. Two thousand more cases since Stevenson's death. The database is what makes the receiver model's reincarnation prediction empirically tractable rather than purely speculative.

Pim van Lommel

The Dutch cardiologist whose prospective study of near-death experiences in cardiac arrest survivors, published in The Lancet in 2001, remains the most methodologically careful evidence for some form of veridical perception during periods of zero EEG activity. Van Lommel's Consciousness Beyond Life (2010) is the clinical case for the receiver model written by a cardiologist who started as a skeptic.

Darold Treffert (1933–2020)

The leading researcher on acquired savant syndrome — cases where head trauma, stroke, or neurodegenerative disease produces sudden onset of previously absent extraordinary abilities (musical, mathematical, artistic). Treffert's archive of cases is, like Stevenson's, what makes the production model untenable as a complete account. The brain that produces consciousness cannot have hidden abilities released by damage. The brain that filters and decodes one can.

Benjamin Libet · Aaron Schurger

The readiness potential and its modern reinterpretation. Libet's experiments (1980s) appeared to show that the brain decides before consciousness does — used by determinists for forty years to argue against free will. Schurger's 2012 stochastic-accumulator model showed that the readiness potential is statistical noise, not deterministic intent. The deterministic reading is dead. What is left is the 300-ms window itself, which the trilogy treats as a symmetric structure rather than a unidirectional one.

Robin Carhart-Harris

The entropic brain hypothesis. Carhart-Harris's psychedelic research at Imperial College London showed that classical psychedelics reduce the activity of the brain's default mode network — that is, they appear to quiet the production machinery rather than activate something new. The receiver-model prediction is that quieting the filter would let more of the underlying signal through. The data are consistent with that prediction.

Biology and the body

Michael Levin

Bioelectric morphogenesis. Levin's work at Tufts has shown that cellular collectives compute, plan, and pursue goals using bioelectric signaling — that "intelligence" is a property biological tissue exhibits at scales far below the nervous system. The implication is enormous: cognition is not exclusive to brains. The trilogy uses Levin's framework to extend the receiver model down to the body, the fascia, and possibly to the single cell.

Gerald Pollack

Exclusion-zone water. Pollack's research at the University of Washington has documented a structured phase of water — gel-like, with anomalous physical properties — that forms at hydrophilic surfaces, including biological tissue. EZ water may be the substrate through which intracellular bioelectric and quantum effects propagate in warm wet biology. The trilogy's argument that the body is more antenna than meat depends on water doing more than current biology says it does.

Anirban Bandyopadhyay

The microtubule coherence laboratory. Bandyopadhyay's experimental claim — terahertz coherent vibrations in microtubule lattices — is the most direct empirical test of the Penrose-Hameroff hypothesis. The work is contested, the dispute is genuine, and the data are still coming in. The trilogy treats microtubule quantum coherence as a serious candidate rather than as proven; the empirical question is open.

Frequency, music, pattern

Ernst Chladni (1756–1827)

The Chladni patterns. A violinist and physicist who showed that vibrating plates organize themselves into geometric patterns according to the frequencies passing through them. The most concrete demonstration that frequency creates form. The trilogy's cymatic motif — Lucía Reyes seeing events as resolving patterns — is a literary extension of Chladni's experimental observation.

Gerald Oster

The 1973 Scientific American paper on binaural beats. Oster showed that the brain detects sub-acoustic frequency differences between the two ears as a third tone — perceived in the brain rather than in the environment. The neurophysiological evidence that the body is a frequency-aware system at scales below conscious perception. Underlies the trilogy's argument that φ-tuning has a body-level effect different from cognitive recognition.

Winfried Otto Schumann (1888–1974)

Predicted and named the Schumann resonance in 1952. The electromagnetic cavity between Earth's surface and the ionosphere oscillates at ~7.83 Hz and its harmonics — frequencies that overlap directly with human alpha and theta brain rhythms. The biological coupling, if real, is the trilogy's most concrete claim about the body as a planetary-scale antenna.

Philosophy, mysticism, and the deeper traditions

Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582)

The most precise phenomenologist of mystical experience in the Western tradition. Teresa's Interior Castle (1577) maps the architecture of contemplative practice in stages so specific that contemporary neuroscience can correlate them with fMRI signatures. Her work is the trilogy's anchor in the Catholic contemplative tradition and one of the cleanest cases against the assumption that mysticism is vague.

The Kashmir Shaivism tradition

Abhinavagupta, Utpaladeva, Kshemaraja. The most sophisticated non-dual metaphysics produced anywhere — the claim that consciousness is the substrate, that the world is its self-recognition, and that liberation is the recovery of that recognition by a localised mode. Structurally identical to the receiver model in nearly every respect, with a thousand-year head start on the formal vocabulary. The trilogy's Limen draws on this tradition heavily.

Rumi (1207–1273)

The ocean in a drop. One line. The whole receiver model is in it.

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)

Amor est velle alicui bonum — to love is to will the good of the other for their own sake. The epigraph of Anima, and the working definition of love throughout the trilogy. Aquinas is not on this site as a metaphysical authority but as the source of the only sentence that says clearly what love actually is. The books spend a great deal of effort earning that sentence.

A closing note

The list above is incomplete and could not be otherwise. Hundreds of researchers, philosophers, contemplatives, physicists, neuroscientists, and quiet thinkers in adjacent fields have contributed pieces. The trilogy's particular synthesis is one literary arrangement of work that took many hundreds of careers to produce. The mistakes in the books are mine. The insights are theirs.

The most accurate one-sentence summary of what this page is for: if any of the trilogy's central ideas seem worth taking seriously, the reader should know that the actual intellectual work was done by other people, and the actual extensions of the work are available on their own pages, in their own books, in the citations on the Reading page. The novels are a way into the conversation. The conversation existed before the novels, and will continue after.

If any of the people on this page happen to find this site and read this entry, please accept the gratitude. I would never have been able to attempt the books without your work.
