Reader companion · the physicists' testimony · consciousness as fundamental

Physics and consciousness as fundamental — the working physicists who said so.

It is a recurring claim of contemporary materialist discourse that the consciousness-as-fundamental position is a fringe metaphysical opinion that the working scientists who built modern physics did not share. The historical record does not support this. A long list of the figures who actually built quantum mechanics, relativity, astrophysics, and quantum information — Max Planck, Erwin Schrödinger, Werner Heisenberg, Wolfgang Pauli, Arthur Eddington, James Jeans, John Archibald Wheeler, Eugene Wigner — wrote at length and in their own names that consciousness is foundational, that the ordinary picture of independent material substance is in some way derivative, and that the strange features of modern physics most parsimoniously fit a substrate-mind metaphysics rather than the materialist default. This essay walks their testimony, citing the published works directly. The framework on this site reads them as the technical-physics complement of the philosophical idealist tradition the companion essay walks.

Companion to the idealist tradition (the philosophical lineage these physicists' positions inhabit), D'Ariano and Faggin (the contemporary information-theoretic continuation), Bohm's implicate order and Stapp's mind-matter framework (the closely-related twentieth-century articulations), the hard problem restated, the measurement problem, What does the wave wave on?, and the Synthesis.

1. The claim, and what counts as evidence for it

What follows is a survey of working physicists of the twentieth century — Nobel laureates and architects of contemporary physics — whose own published writings state that consciousness is foundational and matter derivative, or that the working physicist who looks honestly at what contemporary physics describes is driven toward a substrate-mind picture rather than a substrate-matter picture. The intent is to let each figure speak in their own published words, with the published works named so readers can verify.

Two honest caveats up front. First, several of the most-circulated quotations from these figures (especially the Planck consciousness quote) have provenance discussions that are worth knowing about; the relevant attributions are flagged below. Second, the survey is not a claim that twentieth-century physics proved consciousness is foundational. It is a claim about what a representative sample of the working physicists who built the field actually thought and wrote — which is interesting because it contradicts the contemporary materialist assumption that the founders held the position contemporary materialism holds.

2. Max Planck — consciousness as the substrate of everything we can talk about

Max Planck (1858–1947), the founder of quantum theory (Nobel Prize 1918) and one of the architects of twentieth-century physics, addressed the consciousness question repeatedly in his later writings and interviews. The position he stated, in compressed form, is that consciousness is the foundational fact that any picture of physical reality has to presuppose — that what we mean by matter is always something that appears within consciousness, and that the materialist programme of treating matter as the primary stuff from which consciousness emerges has the dependency relation backwards.

The most-circulated single statement of Planck's position is the following passage, widely attributed to a 1931 interview in The Observer (London):

I regard consciousness as fundamental. I regard matter as derivative from consciousness. We cannot get behind consciousness. Everything that we talk about, everything that we regard as existing, postulates consciousness. — Max Planck, widely cited as quoted in The Observer, London, 25 January 1931

The provenance of this exact wording has been the subject of some scholarly discussion — the original interview text is hard to verify directly — but the substantive position Planck holds, in his own signed work, is unambiguous. In Where Is Science Going? (1932; English translation by James Murphy with a preface by Einstein), Planck states the same position at length, arguing that the working physicist cannot avoid the conclusion that the structure of reality, as physics has come to understand it, requires an underlying mind-like ground that the equations of physics describe but do not exhaust. The position is also developed in his earlier The Universe in the Light of Modern Physics (1931) and in his collected essays.

For the framework's purposes, what matters is that the founder of quantum theory — the figure who introduced the quantum of action and from whose work all subsequent quantum mechanics derived — held the position that consciousness is foundational. The position is not external to the development of contemporary physics. It was held by the figure who started it.

3. Erwin Schrödinger — the singular consciousness and the Vedantic frame

Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961), Nobel laureate (1933) and originator of the wave-mechanics formulation of quantum theory, gave the consciousness question its most fully developed twentieth-century philosophical treatment from inside contemporary physics. Three of his books bear directly on the framework's reading. What Is Life? (1944), a series of public lectures originally given in Dublin, gave biology one of its foundational modern texts (the book that, by his own report, influenced Watson and Crick's work on DNA), and closes with an epilogue explicitly addressing consciousness from a position Schrödinger names as Vedantic. Mind and Matter (1958) is the book-length treatment of the consciousness question. My View of the World (Meine Weltansicht, posthumous 1961) collects his most personal philosophical writing.

The thesis Schrödinger develops across these works is that consciousness is in the singular, of which the plural is unknown. The apparent multiplicity of individual minds is, for Schrödinger, a feature of how the singular consciousness presents itself to itself when localized into the perspective of an individual organism. The metaphysical position is what the Indian philosophical tradition (which Schrödinger read seriously throughout his life) calls Vedanta — the recognition of Atman is Brahman, the individual self as identical at its ground with the universal consciousness. Schrödinger did not present this as ornamentation. He presented it as the position the working physicist who looks honestly at the structure of physical reality is driven toward.

The framework reads Schrödinger as the figure who most explicitly states, from inside the physics community of the mid-twentieth century, the position the receiver model also takes: that consciousness is a singular substrate, that individuated minds are localizations within it, and that the contemplative traditions that have named this for millennia were not pre-scientific superstition but precise reports of what an examined first-person perspective discloses.

4. Werner Heisenberg — Plato, forms, and the Copenhagen reading

Werner Heisenberg (1901–1976), Nobel laureate (1932) and founder of matrix mechanics, developed his own philosophical reading of quantum mechanics most fully in Physics and Philosophy: The Revolution in Modern Science (1958), based on the 1955–1956 Gifford Lectures. The position he articulates is that the entities of contemporary physics are not material objects in the ordinary sense but mathematical structures — forms, in something close to the Platonic sense — and that the question of what matter is, when pushed to the finest scale physics can describe, returns an answer that is closer to mathematical pattern than to substance.

Heisenberg's position is sometimes summarised in the formulation that contemporary physics has decided in favour of Plato over Democritus — that is, in favour of forms over particles. The exact wording of the most-circulated version of this statement varies across sources, but the substantive position is consistently held in his published philosophical work. The Copenhagen interpretation Heisenberg co-developed with Niels Bohr places observer-knowledge at the centre of what quantum mechanics describes, and Heisenberg was clear in his philosophical writing that this was not a temporary epistemic limitation to be removed by future physics but a structural feature of what reality, at the quantum level, actually is.

For the framework's reading, Heisenberg's significance is that he places the founder-physicist's most influential interpretation of quantum mechanics — the Copenhagen interpretation — on a Platonist-idealist metaphysical foundation, not a materialist one. The standard textbook treatment that strips this metaphysics out and presents the formalism as if it were neutrally materialist is a later imposition, not what the figures who built the framework actually thought.

5. Wolfgang Pauli and the Jung collaboration — the depth-psychology bridge

Wolfgang Pauli (1900–1958), Nobel laureate (1945) and architect of the exclusion principle that bears his name, carried on a thirty-year correspondence and intellectual collaboration with Carl Jung that constitutes the most fully documented twentieth-century engagement between a foundational physicist and a foundational depth psychologist. The collaboration is preserved in their published correspondence (in English: Atom and Archetype: The Pauli/Jung Letters, 1932–1958, 2001) and in the jointly authored book The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche (1952), which contained Pauli's essay on Kepler and Jung's essay on synchronicity.

Pauli's position, developed across the correspondence, is that the dualism of matter and mind that classical science assumed cannot be sustained on what contemporary physics has found, and that some deeper unity must underlie both — a position Pauli and Jung developed under the (deliberately ambiguous) name unus mundus, the one world. The synchronicity work, in particular, is Jung's and Pauli's joint attempt to formalise the observation that meaningful coincidences cluster in ways that the strict probability theory their respective fields had been built on does not predict — suggesting an underlying ordering principle that operates across both psyche and matter.

The framework reads the Pauli–Jung collaboration as the most serious twentieth-century working-scientist engagement with the question the receiver model also engages: how should the relation between mind and matter be reframed if the two are not separate substances but expressions of a deeper, shared substrate? Pauli's status as one of the architects of quantum mechanics makes him an unusually weighty witness for the position the framework defends.

6. Arthur Eddington and James Jeans — mind-stuff and the great thought

Two British astrophysicists of the early twentieth century stated the consciousness-as-fundamental position in unusually direct prose, accessible enough to a general readership that the relevant quotations have circulated continuously since.

Arthur Eddington (1882–1944), the astronomer and physicist who led the 1919 solar-eclipse expedition that provided the empirical confirmation of Einstein's general theory of relativity, addressed the consciousness question most fully in The Nature of the Physical World (1928), based on his 1927 Gifford Lectures. The position Eddington articulates is summarised in his often-quoted formulation:

The stuff of the world is mind-stuff. — Arthur Eddington, The Nature of the Physical World (1928), ch. 13

Eddington's argument is that the entities of contemporary physics, when their structure is examined carefully, do not look like the material objects of common-sense intuition. They look like patterns — mathematical structures of relations — and patterns are the kind of thing that mind is naturally placed to recognize. The conclusion Eddington draws is that physics has not described matter in the sense in which the materialist programme intends the term, but rather a structure of mind-recognizable patterns whose underlying substrate is closer to mind than to matter as the materialist had imagined it.

James Jeans (1877–1946), the astrophysicist and mathematician, made the same case in The Mysterious Universe (1930), the popular-audience book that, on its release, became one of the best-selling science books of the early twentieth century. The book's most-quoted single line:

The Universe begins to look more like a great thought than like a great machine. — James Jeans, The Mysterious Universe (1930), ch. 5

Jeans's argument runs in parallel to Eddington's. The universe contemporary physics describes is not the clockwork mechanism nineteenth-century physics had imagined. It is a structure of mathematical relations whose features, when honestly described, are closer to the structure of thought than to the structure of mechanical interaction. The materialist picture of independent matter, Jeans argues, was a useful approximation for a particular period of physics; it does not survive contact with what contemporary physics has actually found.

7. John Archibald Wheeler — «it from bit» and the participatory universe

John Archibald Wheeler (1911–2008) was the American physicist whose work spans the development of general relativity (he coined the term black hole), the foundations of quantum mechanics, and the contemporary information-theoretic reconstructions of physics. His late-career work develops two positions central to the framework's reading.

The first is the slogan it from bit — the proposal, developed in Wheeler's 1989 paper Information, Physics, Quantum: The Search for Links, that every it — every particle, every field, every spacetime continuum — derives in some way from the binary distinctions registered by acts of observation. Reality, on Wheeler's reading, is not informational at the surface and material underneath; it is informational down to the bottom, with information as the substrate from which physical instantiation emerges. The position is the engineer-physicist's articulation of the informational ontology the framework treats elsewhere as central (see Information as the foundation for the standalone essay).

The second is the participatory universe, the position developed across Wheeler's late work that the act of observation is constitutive of reality, not merely descriptive of it. The delayed-choice experiment Wheeler proposed in the 1970s and 1980s — in which the experimenter's choice about how to measure a photon affects its history backwards in time — is for Wheeler not a paradox to be explained away but a structural fingerprint of what reality actually is: an ongoing co-construction by observers and what they observe. The clip catalogue's Clip 31 (James Gates) on error-correcting codes in supersymmetric field equations is the contemporary mathematical evidence for the informational substrate Wheeler proposed.

The framework reads Wheeler as the figure who most clearly states, from inside late-twentieth-century theoretical physics, the position the contemporary information-theoretic reconstructions of consciousness (D'Ariano, Faggin) develop. Wheeler is the bridge from the founding generation of quantum mechanics to the present.

8. Eugene Wigner — observer-induced collapse and Wigner's friend

Eugene Wigner (1902–1995), Nobel laureate (1963) and one of the architects of the application of group theory to quantum mechanics, addressed the consciousness question directly in his essay Remarks on the Mind-Body Question (1961), published in the symposium volume The Scientist Speculates edited by I.J. Good. The essay's position is the most explicit statement, from a major working physicist of the mid-twentieth century, that consciousness has to be assigned a constitutive role in quantum mechanics itself.

Wigner's argument runs as follows. The wavefunction evolves smoothly and deterministically according to the Schrödinger equation. The moment a measurement happens, the wavefunction collapses to a definite outcome. The question of what counts as a measurement — what physical process, that is, can be the agent of collapse — turns out, when pursued honestly, to keep regressing: instruments are themselves physical systems describable by quantum mechanics, observers' brains are themselves physical systems describable by quantum mechanics, and there is no place along the chain where the Schrödinger evolution stops applying. Wigner's conclusion was that the only place where the regress terminates is at the point of conscious observation — the place where physical description gives way to subjective experience — and that consciousness therefore has to be assigned a fundamental role in the description of physical reality.

The associated thought experiment, Wigner's friend, asks what happens when two observers (Wigner and a friend inside a sealed laboratory) measure the same quantum system. Until Wigner opens the laboratory door, the friend has registered a definite measurement outcome and Wigner has not. Whose perspective gives the correct description of the system in the interim? The thought experiment has had a major contemporary revival (the Frauchiger–Renner result, 2018, gives a formal proof that consistent quantum mechanics requires giving up one of several intuitive assumptions about shared reality), and the contemporary Virk clip on entanglement as the engineer's optimization technique (Clip 35) takes Wigner's friend as the architectural anchor for the engineer's reading of what shared rendering would have to involve.

Wigner himself, in his later years, moved away from the strong observer-collapse position on technical grounds, but the structural argument his 1961 essay made — that consciousness cannot be cleanly factored out of a complete description of quantum mechanics — remains live. The framework reads him as the architect of the question contemporary quantum information continues to ask.

9. The convergence and what the framework takes

The convergence across these eight figures is what the framework treats as the empirical anchor of the consciousness-as-fundamental position. None of them was a fringe figure. All were Nobel laureates or architects of contemporary physics. Their published positions, in their own books and papers, hold that consciousness is foundational, that the materialist picture of independent matter is an approximation that does not survive serious examination, and that the most parsimonious reading of what contemporary physics describes is closer to a mind-substrate metaphysics than to a matter-substrate metaphysics.

What the framework takes from this lineage is not authority — physics is not settled by who said what — but rather the displacement of a misimpression that has done a lot of damage to the contemporary conversation. The misimpression is that the consciousness-as-fundamental position is a fringe metaphysical option held by people who do not understand contemporary physics. The published record is that the position was held, at considerable length and in their own signed work, by the figures who built contemporary physics. The contemporary materialist consensus is a later imposition by figures who came after the founders. The founders themselves, on the whole, did not hold it.

The contemporary technical descendants of this lineage — D'Ariano and Faggin's informational reconstruction, Bohm's implicate order, Stapp's mind-matter framework, Hoffman's interface theory — continue the conversation the founders started. The framework on this site, and the trilogy that gives it literary form, sit, with appropriate humility, inside that conversation.
